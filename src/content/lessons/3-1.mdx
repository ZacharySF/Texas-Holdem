<Hook>
  Deal two cards from A, B, C, D. You want the hand containing A and B,
  whichever arrives first. An ordered tree counts A then B and B then A
  separately. Your hand question calls both of those the same success. How do
  you remove the duplication without losing real hands?
</Hook>

<Idea>
### Group the ordered paths

Start with the ordered count you already know: four choices for the first card and three for the second. List those paths before trying a shortcut.

<Orders size={4} take={2} />

Put A then B beside B then A. They describe one unordered hand, the set containing A and B. Put A then C beside C then A, and continue. Every set of two distinct cards has two internal orders. No set has only one order, and no set has three. The groups therefore all have the same size.

To count groups, divide the number of paths by the size of a group. Division here removes repeated descriptions of the **same** chosen cards. It does not mean discarding half the physical cards, and it does not mean dividing the probability by two merely because two cards were dealt.

<CombinationExamples />

The first notebook groups the small deck's ordered paths. The second applies the same argument to a standard deck. In each case the denominator accounts for rearranging the selected cards, rather than changing which cards were selected.

### Why the divisor is a factorial

Now choose three distinct cards. A particular chosen set can be arranged with three choices for its first position, two for its second, and one for its last. Every unordered three-card set therefore occurs that many times in the ordered tree. Divide by the factorial of the number selected.

For k selected cards, the number of internal orders is k factorial. The ordered-selection count is n factorial divided by the factorial of n minus k. Dividing that ordered count by k factorial gives:

<Formula tex={'\\binom{n}{k}=\\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}'} />

Read the stacked symbol as **n choose k**. It is also written C(n, k). A **combination** is an unordered selection of k distinct objects from n available objects. The symbol is a compact name for the grouping argument, not a reason to skip it.

### Keep both favorable and total counts consistent

In the small-deck example, the target hand \{A, B\} has two favorable ordered paths. The full sample space has all the ordered paths listed above. If you switch to unordered hands, the favorable count becomes one and the total is also divided by the same internal-order count. The probability is unchanged.

A common mistake is to use one favorable unordered hand over the total number of **ordered** paths. That numerator and denominator count different kinds of objects. Write “paths” or “sets” beside both counts before dividing them.

### Choosing what remains is another description

Choosing k cards determines the n minus k cards left behind. The two choices describe the same partition of the available cards. This explains why choosing a small number and choosing its complement have equal combination counts. Use that symmetry as a check, not as a substitute for defining the event.

The formula also gives one way to choose no cards, and one way to choose all available cards. Both are sensible boundary cases: each question has exactly one set as its answer.

### When the division argument needs care

Our cards are distinct and are selected without replacement. If a model permits repeated objects or merges objects into rank patterns, its groups may need different counting. The reliable question is always: “How many ordered descriptions belong to each object I actually want to count?”

  Your two hole cards form an unordered hand. Use combinations for that

  arrival order matters to the question: a final board set and a sequence of

<QuantCorner>
  Selecting a basket of instruments can be an unordered problem, while executing
  a sequence of trades is ordered. Dividing out orderings is justified only for
  the first kind of question. A clear counting unit prevents the same scenario
  from receiving extra weight just because it has several descriptions.
</QuantCorner>
