<Hook>
  One card is drawn from a fresh deck, and you are waiting for a heart. The
  answer might be written as a fraction, a percentage, “one in” a number of
  trials, or odds against. These are four descriptions of one probability, not
  four different predictions.
</Hook>

<Idea>
### Keep the event fixed

There are thirteen hearts among the physical cards in a full deck. Reduce the favorable-over-total fraction first. Reducing changes the size of the units you use to describe the ratio; it does not discard possible cards from the experiment.

A **percent** expresses a ratio per hundred. Multiply the probability by one hundred to get the percentage number, then attach the percent sign. The first small derivation below carries out that multiplication. To move back to a probability, divide the percentage number by one hundred.

### “One in” includes the successful outcome

For a nonzero probability P, “one in N” uses N equal to one divided by P. Imagine scaling the favorable count to one and scaling the total by the same amount. The total includes that one favorable outcome.

This is a frequency description over repeated experiments, not a schedule. A one-in estimate does not mean that successes follow a fixed schedule. Nor does a run of misses make a restored full deck more likely to produce a heart on the next trial.

### Odds against count failures separately

Odds against compare unfavorable outcomes to favorable outcomes. If P is the favorable fraction, the unfavorable fraction is one minus P. Divide the latter by the former. The result is written N : 1 against: N unfavorable units for each favorable unit.

The “one in” denominator is one larger than the odds-against number because the former includes the favorable unit. If someone quotes odds, ask whether they mean odds against or odds in favor. Here the display always labels odds **against**.

### Read rounding honestly

The reduced fraction is exact. Decimal displays may be rounded, as signaled by the approximation symbol when it is used in a derivation. A displayed percent should not silently replace the exact fraction in a later calculation. Our code keeps the fraction and only rounds the presentation.

### The endpoints

An impossible event has no favorable outcomes. Its probability is zero, and a finite “one in N” number cannot describe it: dividing one by zero has no finite answer. The infinity symbol in our display marks that limit, not a waiting-time guarantee.

A certain event contains the whole sample space. Its probability is one. There are no unfavorable outcomes, so its odds against are zero to one. These statements assume the stated experiment: a standard deck has four suits, so a fifth suit is impossible within that model.

<AtTheTable>
  Translate quoted odds into the same format before comparing them. A one-in
  number and the same number quoted as odds against are not the same
  probability. When later chapters compare the chance of winning with the price
  of a call, this conversion prevents a unit mistake from turning into a chip
  mistake.
