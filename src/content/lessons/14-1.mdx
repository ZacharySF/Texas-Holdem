<Hook>

The same positive-EV all-in can win or lose fifty chips. How far do individual outcomes lie from the average?

</Hook>

<Idea>

Variance is the average squared distance from the mean. Subtract the mean from each payoff, square the difference, weight by its probability, then add. Squaring keeps positive and negative deviations from cancelling. It also means variance has squared-chip units. Standard deviation is the square root of variance, returning to chips.

For two outcomes, expand the squares and simplify: variance equals the expected squared payoff minus the square of the expected payoff. The computed example shows both the mean and spread for the same payoff model used in the EV chapter. Larger spread does not change the average; it changes how unpredictable an individual result is.

For independent hands, variances add. Standard deviations do not: the square root is taken after adding variances. A running estimate from data uses squared deviations and divides by one fewer than the observation count to estimate the population variance. Welford’s update maintains that calculation without retaining every sample or subtracting two huge nearly equal sums.

A positive-EV decision can be too volatile for a small play-money bankroll. Use both expected value and a plausible spread when interpreting short runs.

</AtTheTable>

<QuantCorner>

Standard deviation measures volatility; drawdowns also depend on the order of results. Two paths can have the same final total and very different experiences along the way.
