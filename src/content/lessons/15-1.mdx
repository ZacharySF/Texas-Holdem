<Hook>

Run repeated sets of all-ins, then increase the number of trials inside each set. Individual outcomes still vary, but their average settles.

</Hook>

<Idea>

The law of large numbers says that, under suitable conditions such as independent identically distributed trials with a finite mean, the sample average approaches the population mean as the sample grows. It does not say a losing streak must be repaid by wins. New trials keep their own probabilities; their accumulated average becomes less sensitive to any one result.

For binary wins, the sample average is the win count divided by the number of trials. Its expectation remains p for every sample size. Its variance is p times one minus p divided by sample size, because independent indicator variances add and dividing a sum by n divides its variance by n squared. The standard deviation of these averages is called the standard error.

The simulator repeats entire sets and plots their averages. Change the set size to distinguish two operations: more sets improve the estimate of the distribution; more hands per set narrow that distribution. Watching a single running path is useful, but it cannot show every possible path you might have received.

<QuantCorner>

A backtest average may stabilize when observations provide new independent information. Repeating nearly identical correlated trades does not create the same evidence as independent data.
