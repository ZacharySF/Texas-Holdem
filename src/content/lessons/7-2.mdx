<Hook>

You hold Qs Qd. The flop contains an ace, or a king, or both. Separately, it might be rainbow, two-tone, or monotone. How do these descriptions divide up the possible boards?

</Hook>

<Idea>

Pocket queens leave eight overcards: four kings and four aces. Count the flops with none of those eight cards and subtract from all flops. This handles “ace or king” without counting a board with both twice. Multiplying three times a one-card overcard chance would double-count boards with several overcards.

The complement automatically includes every disjoint possibility in the desired event: one overcard, two overcards, or three. It does not assert that an opponent holds an ace or king. An overcard on the board is a change in the public information, not proof that your pair has been beaten. Against some hands queens remain ahead; against others the board changes the likely winner dramatically.

Suit texture is another description. Monotone means all three flop cards share one suit. Two-tone means exactly two share a suit and the third has another suit. Rainbow means all three suits differ. Those cases are disjoint and exhaustive: exactly one, two, or three distinct suits must appear on a three-card board. Their exact probabilities add to one.

Rank texture is separate. A paired board means at least two flop cards share a rank; in this lesson that includes trips. A board can be both paired and two-tone. It cannot be paired and monotone because a deck has only one card of each rank in each suit. When combining rank and suit claims, inspect their overlap rather than assuming that texture labels are independent.

<CourseExamples />

Known hole cards change the suit counts. With Qs Qd removed, two suits have one fewer available card than the other two. You cannot use four identical full-suit counts in every conditional calculation. The exact enumerator visits every three-card subset of the remaining deck, counts its distinct suits and repeated ranks, and increments the selected event once. The notebook’s N is that favorable count. This is a small enough count to check exhaustively, and the same predicate is then tested by random dealing in the worker.

A pencil count can check the monotone total independently: for each suit, choose three from the cards of that suit still available, then add the four disjoint suit cases. For two-tone, choose the doubled suit, choose two remaining cards from it, and choose one card from all the other suits. For rainbow, choose three different suits and multiply their remaining-card counts. These are the same multiplication and addition rules from chapters two and four, applied after card removal.

The practice set varies your pocket pair’s rank and the suit pattern of the starting hand. Notice that an ace pair has no overcards, while a lower pair has many. The machinery is unchanged; only the favorable card set changes. Use the exact count to test a rough verbal estimate before relying on it.

<AtTheTable>

Read rank texture and suit texture together, then count what your opponent could actually hold. A monotone board changes flush possibilities; a paired board introduces full-house possibilities. An overcard alone is not a reason to assume you have lost.

</AtTheTable>

<QuantCorner>

Several labels can describe the same observation, so adding their frequencies may double-count cases. Explicitly defining disjoint groups helps when grouping market outcomes too. Conditioning on a starting position can also change the balance between those groups.
