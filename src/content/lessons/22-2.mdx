<Hook>

You face a river bet from a range containing only unbeatable value hands and hands you beat. Which mix makes calling and folding equally valuable?

</Hook>

<Idea>

Let q be the fraction of bluffs among bets you face. Calling a bluff wins the old pot plus the opponent’s bet. Calling value loses your call. Set the probability-weighted sum to zero and solve for q. The denominator contains the original pot and both bets. This is a fraction of the betting range, not the bluff-to-value ratio and not the bettor’s required fold frequency. These are three different questions.

A mixed strategy assigns probabilities to actions at the same information set. Mixing does not mean forgetting what happened. The information set contains your own card and the public betting history, while keeping the opponent’s card hidden. At equilibrium, any action used with positive frequency has the same value against the opponent’s strategy; otherwise shifting probability to a better action would improve your result.

The playable AKQ game is three-card Kuhn poker relabeled Q, K, A. Each player antes one, receives a distinct private card, and may check or make one bet of one. After a bet the response is call or fold; there are no raises. Check-check reveals cards, as does a call. The larger rank wins. Payoffs include the ante. The game enumerates all deals and all public action paths.

The displayed equilibrium belongs to this bounded game. We verify it by holding one player’s policy fixed and trying every deterministic policy for the other. Such a best response must use the same action whenever its information set is identical; choosing based on the hidden card would cheat. Neither player can gain by deviating, even though the first player’s equilibrium payoff is negative. Fair rules need not give equal value to different positions.
