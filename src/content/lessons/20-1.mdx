<Hook>

You shuffle three labeled cards by swapping each position with any position. Every swap feels random. Does every final ordering have the same number of paths?

</Hook>

<Idea>

A pseudorandom generator is a deterministic machine with a small internal state. Our xoshiro128\*\* generator changes four unsigned words using shifts, exclusive-or, and rotation. The seed fixes its initial state, so it fixes every subsequent draw. A seed from the browser’s cryptographic generator makes a new experiment unpredictable before it starts; recording it makes the experiment repeatable afterward. A PRNG is not itself a cryptographic commitment.

A generated word has a power-of-two number of possible values. Dividing a word by a requested bound and keeping the remainder is biased unless the bound divides the word count. Try eight equally likely labels and three buckets: distribute the labels cyclically, then notice that some buckets receive an extra label. Rejection sampling discards the incomplete last block and tries again. Every retained bucket has exactly the same number of labels. The Shuffle Lab enumerates both assignments.

Fisher–Yates fixes the final position by choosing uniformly from every remaining card, then fixes the preceding position from the cards still available. A particular ordering corresponds to exactly one sequence of choices. Multiply the successive choice counts to get the factorial number of equally likely paths. The naive method keeps disturbing positions it already visited. Its equally likely paths do not divide evenly among final orders. The table and notebook count those paths, not guessed frequencies.

The chi-square statistic adds squared observed-minus-expected counts divided by expected counts. Squaring prevents positive and negative gaps from cancelling; dividing scales each gap by its expected size. With six orders and their total fixed, five counts can vary freely, hence five degrees of freedom. The displayed tail probability uses the large-sample chi-square approximation under a uniform-order null hypothesis. It is not the probability that the shuffle is fair. Small expected counts weaken that approximation; the exact path count proves the bias without a statistical test.
