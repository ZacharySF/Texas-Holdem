<Hook>

An opponent bets. Strong hands may bet more often, but weak hands can bet too. How much should the action change your belief?

</Hook>

<Idea>

Bayes’ rule follows from counting the observed branch of a probability tree. Let A mean a strong hand and B the observed bet. Multiply the prior chance of A by the chance of betting when strong. Do the same for weak hands, then add both products to obtain the total chance of observing a bet. Divide the strong-and-bet branch by that total.

The notebook uses supplied illustrative branch weights. In the simulator, first choose strong or weak from the prior, then sample the action conditional on that choice, and keep the trials with a bet. The retained strong fraction estimates the posterior. This rejection experiment makes the conditioning visible instead of treating a bet as certain evidence of strength.

For many combos, multiply every prior combo weight by its action likelihood, remove blocked combos, and normalize. The likelihood model is essential: a calling station and an aggressive player need not produce the same posterior after the same action. If your model assigns zero probability to the observation, the conditional distribution is undefined; do not quietly divide by zero or invent a posterior.

<QuantCorner>

Bayesian updating combines a prior belief with the likelihood of new evidence. A precise posterior can still be misleading when the likelihood model is poorly chosen.
