<Hook>

You start with five betting units and stop at either zero or ten. Each independent even-money trial changes your balance by one. How often do you hit zero first?

</Hook>

<Idea>

Let u(i) be the chance of reaching zero before the target when starting with i units. The boundary values are one at zero and zero at the target. From an interior balance, condition on the next trial: u(i) equals p times u(i plus one) plus one minus p times u(i minus one). This recurrence expresses the whole future using its first step.

For a fair trial, adjacent differences are equal, so the solution is a straight line between the two boundary values. For a biased trial, the differences form a geometric progression with ratio (one minus p) divided by p. Summing that progression and applying the two boundaries gives the exact formula implemented by the calculator. If every step wins, ruin is impossible; if every step loses, ruin is certain. Handle the fair case separately because the geometric formula otherwise divides zero by zero.

This is eventual ruin before a target, not ruin by a fixed hand count and not an unbounded walk. There are no withdrawals, costs, variable stakes, or changing probabilities. The worker follows each independent path until a boundary and compares the frequency of ruined paths with the exact fraction. A computational path cap reports an error rather than quietly treating unfinished paths as safe.

The earlier bankroll simulator uses a different model of normally distributed block results and observes ruin at block endpoints. It may miss a crossing inside a block. Fractional betting is different again: a fraction below one leaves positive mathematical wealth after any finite sequence of losses, but minimum chip sizes and living expenses can create a practical failure threshold.
