<Hook>

Suppose each all-in has a supplied win chance of <SuppliedChance /> and no ties. What is the chance of winning every one of five?

</Hook>

<Idea>

A Bernoulli trial has two outcomes: success or failure. Write its success chance as p and failure chance as one minus p. Independent trials mean that the previous result does not change the next trial’s chance. Freshly shuffled deals can support that model; changing opponents and hand strengths often do not. Here the chance is supplied and fixed deliberately.

One specified sequence containing k wins and n minus k losses has probability p to the kth power times one minus p to the remaining power. Multiplication follows the path through all trials. There are C(n,k) different placements of those wins. They are disjoint sequences, so multiply the path probability by their count. This gives the binomial distribution. For exactly sixteen wins among twenty trials, the coefficient matters; for winning all five, there is only one successful ordering.

The expected number of wins is n times p: add each trial’s success indicator, which is one for a win and zero for a loss. Expected count is an average over repeated sets, not the most certain result of a particular set. The event builder lets you compose card events across freshly restored decks and ask for exactly or at least a chosen number of successes. It uses exact card-event probabilities in the binomial calculation and deals the events again in simulation.

<AtTheTable>

Do not turn a strong favorite into a promise to win a short sequence. Check whether identical independent trials are a reasonable model.

<QuantCorner>

Repeated independent trades with a fixed success chance produce the same binomial counts. Real trades can share information and market exposure, so independence must be argued rather than assumed.
