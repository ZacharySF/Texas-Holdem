<Hook>

A single all-in has only two possible outcomes. Why can averages of many all-ins form a smooth-looking mound?

</Hook>

<Idea>

The central limit theorem describes the shape of a standardized sum or average under conditions such as independent identically distributed observations with finite nonzero variance. Subtract its true mean and divide by its standard error; as sample size grows, its distribution approaches the standard normal bell curve. It concerns repeated samples, not a promise about one observed path.

The original outcomes need not be bell-shaped. Binary all-ins are an extreme example: individual values sit at two points, while their averages occupy many possible fractions. Small samples or rare success chances can remain skewed and discrete. The normal approximation improves only when enough of both outcomes can occur; it never makes impossible values possible.

Change the sample size and compare the histogram. Its center stays near the supplied mean; its width shrinks. A bankroll simulator may use normal increments directly as a model of aggregate hands, but that is an assumption about the approximation. Large bets, dependence, and changing opponents can create tails or clusters that its simple model misses.

Treat a bell-shaped approximation as a tool with conditions. Check the scale and whether rare, large outcomes dominate before relying on its tail estimates.

Aggregated returns can look more regular than individual payoffs. Heavy tails and dependence can make a normal approximation unreliable exactly where risk estimates matter most.
