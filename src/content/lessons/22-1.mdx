<Hook>

There are 100 chips in the pot. You consider betting 50 with no chance to win if called. How often must your opponent fold?

</Hook>

<Idea>

Measure incremental results from the decision point. Folding or giving up adds zero. When the bluff succeeds, you win the current pot P. When called with no winning chance, you lose your added bet B. If f is the fold chance, expected profit is fP minus (one minus f)B. Set that expression to zero, collect the two terms containing f, and divide by P plus B. The notebook substitutes actual chips.

Minimum defense frequency is one minus this break-even fold frequency. It is a baseline that stops this zero-equity bluff from making an immediate profit under the toy assumptions. It is not a command to call that fraction of every actual range: blockers, value density, future betting, and the bettor’s equity can change the strategic problem.

A semi-bluff retains equity when called. After matching bets, the final pot is P plus twice B. With conditional called equity e, the called branch is e times that final pot minus B. Weight this branch by the call chance, then add the fold branch fP. The equity must be against the range that calls. Averaging against all hands before the opponent folds usually answers the wrong question.

Use a chance tree with a fold branch and a call branch. Within the call branch, add win, tie, and loss pot shares. This separates two routes to profit: winning immediately and recovering part of the pot at showdown. The model ends at showdown with no future betting and excludes rake.
