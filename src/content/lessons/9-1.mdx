<Hook>

How many aces might appear among seven cards? Counting each possible ordering is possible, but needlessly long.

</Hook>

<Idea>

Let N be the population size, K the number of target cards, n the cards drawn, and k the target cards actually drawn. A particular seven-card set is equally likely to any other. To obtain exactly k aces, choose k from the four aces and choose the remaining cards from the non-aces. Multiply those independent counting choices; then divide by the number of all seven-card sets. This is called the hypergeometric distribution: a table assigning a probability to every possible target count when sampling without replacement.

The count k can range only from zero to the smaller of K and n. If too few non-targets remain to fill the hand, that count is impossible too. Summing the feasible counts must give one because every hand has exactly one ace count. For at least one ace, either add the separate positive counts or subtract the zero-ace count from one. The simulator uses the actual shrinking population, not repeated draws with replacement.

To ask whether anyone among three named opponents holds an ace, fix your own cards first, remove them, then draw six opposing cards as one set. Its composition has the same hypergeometric count even though you later divide that set among seats. This shortcut answers whether anyone has an ace; it does not tell you which seat has it, or whether an ace beats your hand. Change the population and success counts when the board exposes more cards.
