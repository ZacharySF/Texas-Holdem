<Hook>

You have not seen pocket aces for a long time. Does that make aces due on the next fresh deal?

</Hook>

<Idea>

Let T count hands up to and including the first success. To wait exactly t hands, fail t minus one times and then succeed. To succeed by hand t, subtract the chance of failing all t attempts from one. This is the geometric distribution. With independent fresh deals, its remaining waiting time does not shorten because of past failures.

The average waiting time can be derived without an infinite list. Write E for the unknown average. Every attempt consumes one hand. With chance one minus p, it fails and the process starts again with average E still to go. Therefore E equals one plus (one minus p) times E. Rearranging gives E equals one divided by p. The aces simulation counts the successful hand, matching this definition. Its distribution has a long right tail; its average need not resemble one typical wait.

For many independent opportunities each having a tiny chance, the Poisson distribution approximates the count of rare successes. Let lambda be the expected count. Its probability of k events is e to minus lambda, times lambda to the kth power, divided by k factorial. Here e is a number defined by the limit of (1 plus one divided by n) to the nth power as n grows. It arises because the no-event chance (1 minus lambda divided by n) to the nth power approaches e to minus lambda. Powers of e are evaluated numerically; no logarithms are needed here. Compare the approximation with the exact binomial calculation below before using it.

<AtTheTable>

A long dry spell does not improve the next independent deal. Use a waiting-time model to judge patience, not to choose a larger wager.

</AtTheTable>

<QuantCorner>

Waiting for rare fills or failures uses the same distributions. A Poisson approximation assumes independent opportunities and a stable rate; clustered events violate that mechanism.

</QuantCorner>
