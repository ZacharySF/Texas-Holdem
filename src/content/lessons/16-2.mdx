<Hook>

After an unlucky set of all-ins, is the observed win count surprising under the chance you assumed?

</Hook>

<Idea>

A null hypothesis supplies the distribution being tested. Here it says the trials are independent with a specified win chance. A two-sided exact binomial p-value adds probabilities of counts no more likely under that null than the observed count. It measures how extreme the observation is under that model. It is not the probability that the null is true, and it is not the probability that you are playing badly.

The computed example lists the observed count and the resulting sum. A low value can signal an incorrect chance, dependence, selective reporting, or simply an unusual sample. Repeatedly inspecting results and stopping only after a surprising result changes the procedure’s false-alarm rate. Decide a sample size and test rule before seeing the outcomes when you want a conventional test interpretation.

Guess the Equity records a forecast before revealing the answer. The Brier score is the squared difference between the forecast probability and a binary outcome, averaged across forecasts. On tied pots we sample a pot unit, so its binary winning probability equals equity. Calibration groups similar forecasts and compares their average with the observed frequency; small groups need intervals. Good calibration alone does not imply useful discrimination between strong and weak hands.
