<Hook>

You start with Qs Qd. A flop containing another queen looks very different from a flop that misses both queens. How do you count those boards without confusing a draw with a made hand?

</Hook>

<Idea>

Start by fixing the two hole cards. There are fifty cards left and an unordered flop has three cards. Use the same denominator for every flop event below, but redefine the favorable group for each question. Starting-hand frequencies used the original full deck; conditional flop frequencies do not.

Before fixing the hole cards, count physical starting combos rather than equally weighting hand names. A pair, a suited non-pair, and an offsuit non-pair partition the possible starting hands. Pocket aces are a subset of the pair event.

<StartingFacts />

For a pocket pair, two cards of its rank remain. Count flops containing neither of them, then subtract from all flops. This event is “the pocket rank appears at least once”: it includes one matching card and both matching cards. The customary set-mining number below uses this definition. A flop of three cards of some other rank can make a full house with your pair without hitting your rank; that separate event is not included. Saying “set or better” without defining the event would hide this distinction.

For an unpaired hand such as As Kd, three aces and three kings remain. The event “pair at least one hole rank” means the flop contains any of those six cards; it also includes stronger matches such as trips. Count the flops avoiding all six and use the complement. To pair both hole ranks exactly once, choose one of three aces, one of three kings, and one card of neither rank. The ranks identify those roles, so there is no extra division by the order of ace and king.

With Ah Kh, eleven hearts remain. An exact flush draw means precisely two more hearts and one non-heart on the flop. Choose the two hearts as a set and then choose the non-heart. A made flush means all three flop cards are hearts. These two events are disjoint; a made flush is not called an exact draw here. The made-flush event includes a straight flush if those ranks happen to form one.

<CourseExamples />

Straight events depend on ranks as well as suits. For 8h 9h, a flop 5c 6d 7s makes a straight; 6c 7d Ks leaves a one-card straight draw. A gutshot is included in the latter event, and an already made straight is excluded. We count it by visiting every unordered flop, collecting the five visible ranks, checking that no straight is already made, then asking whether adding any one rank completes a run. The wheel treats ace as low; no other wrapping is allowed. Each qualifying flop is counted once even if several different cards complete it.

The last example asks whether the pocket rank appears anywhere on the full five-card board. Its denominator counts five-card boards, not flops. More cards offer more chances to hit, but you do not necessarily get to see all of them for the price of one call. Keep the event’s time horizon separate from a betting decision.

<AtTheTable>

Before calling to set-mine, distinguish flopping your rank from eventually seeing it on the river. A flush draw is not a made flush, and pairing a hole rank is not necessarily enough to beat an opponent. Price decisions against the actual event and the later betting still required.

</AtTheTable>

<QuantCorner>

Conditional counting asks what remains possible after a starting position is fixed. A trading setup can similarly change the relevant sample space. Comparing differently defined outcomes or different time horizons can make otherwise correct numbers misleading.
